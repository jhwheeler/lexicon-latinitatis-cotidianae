# Basic words: shapes, boy, colourful, grandparents (23 Nov 2026)

Jack asked for the shapes ("that seems super basic") and for any other basic word missing in the same way. A list of 956 basic English words (shapes, colours, numbers, family, body, animals, food, house, clothes, common verbs and adjectives) was checked against the lexicon: 943 had an entry. The 13 gaps, with their near neighbours, went to one Opus researcher (report only); Claude (Fable) vetted the report and applied it. All records are drafts.

## What was added

23 new records: circle (the shape), oval, diamond (the shape), pyramid, semicircle, spiral, sphere, cylinder, cube (the shape), cone (the shape), cross (the shape), crescent, arc, hexagon, pentagon, polygon, diagonal, horizontal, vertical, pointed, boy, colourful, and grandparents.

3 records extended: heart (the shape, to draw), blue (light, pale, sky, deep, and navy blue), and yogurt (the spelling "yoghurt").

## Calls for Jack to judge

- **Diamond.** *scutula* leads (Vitr. 7.1.4, of floor tiles; Tac. Agr. 10, of Britain's shape). *rhombus* as a figure of geometry is in Lewis & Short only from the land surveyors and Martianus Capella, so it is an alternative marked late. If the word people use is to lead, they change places.
- **Crescent.** *lūnula* leads (Plaut. Epid. 640, an ornament); *lūnātus* is the adjective. Of the moon itself: *lūna bicornis* (Hor. C.S. 35).
- **Pentagon and polygon.** We have found no classical noun for either: *pentagōnium* and *polygōnum* are late. The classical adjectives are *multangulus* (Lucretius) and *polygōnius* (Vitruvius); for five corners only the late *quīnquangulus*. Hexagon is classical (*hexagōnum*, Col. 5.2.10; *sexangulus*, Ovid).
- **Vertical and horizontal.** Vertical is the phrase *ad perpendiculum* (Cicero, Cato, Caesar), with *ērēctus* and *dīrēctus* beside it; horizontal is *lībrātus* (Vitruvius), with *ad lībellam* and Traupman's *trānsversus*. The answer-kinds report counts vertical as an adjective answered by an adverb, which it is.
- **Oval.** *ōvātus* (Pliny) leads. As a bare noun *ōvātum* is ours; *ellipsis* in this sense is modern (Morgan–Owens, from Egger). *ovālis* is marked to avoid: classically it is the crown of an ovation, with a short *o*.
- **Cross.** *crux* leads on Traupman's use for the suit of clubs and on the Christian sense; classically the word is the gibbet, and we have found no classical place where it names a shape. *decussis* is the classical X. The lexicon still has no record for the Cross itself.
- **Grandparents.** *avus et avia* leads; the plural *avī* (Plin. Ep. 5.6.6) is the alternative, since it also means grandfathers and forefathers.
- **Light and dark blue.** By comparison: *caeruleus dīlūtior*, *caeruleus obscūrior*, on Gellius's *dīlūtior* of a colour (2.26.14) and *obscūrius* of the sea (2.30.11).
- **Heart shape.** We have found no classical place where *cor* names a drawn shape; the examples are ours.

## Dropped or changed in vetting

- Not found in the corpus, so dropped: Varro's dice in Gell. 1.20.4 (cube), a line of Prudentius (cross), Gell. 17.8.4 (boy), Vitr. 6.3.3 and 6.8.7 (diagonal; Vitr. 9 praef. 5 has both *diagōnālis* and *diagōnios* and is kept).
- Hor. C. 2.19.3–4 keeps its edition's *discentīs* and *aurīs*.
- *ovālis* is written with the short *o* of Lewis & Short; the researcher had given it the long vowel of *ōvum*.
- Five composed examples were reworked (a tail after a finished clause, *quasi* with an indicative).
