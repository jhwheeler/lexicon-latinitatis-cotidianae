# Coinages only where needed: the second pass, half B (15 November 2026)

The editor's principle (13 November 2026): a word or phrase we came up with (a term whose `period` is `ours`) belongs in the lexicon only when it is needed: when no author of any period has a word for the concept, or a circumlocution has a really good reason. The first pass (`notes/coinages-2026-11-13.md`) took the coinages that stood beside an attested term. This pass takes the records in which every term is ours, so that there was no attested word on the page at all. The records were divided between two sessions; this file covers the second half.

## Scope

109 records (from *metro train* to *zip line* in the list of records whose only terms are ours), with 124 terms, all marked ours before the pass. The other half is in the other session's notes.

## Method

Each record was read, and then searched on 15 November 2026 with `scripts/lookup.py`:

- **The English**: the headword, each of its `also` names and, where they are few, synonyms (the words are listed at the end of this file), in Traupman, Morgan–Owens (the copy of the *Adumbratio* in the latinitas `refs/`), and Lewis & Short's glosses (`en`).
- **Andresian**: the word lists with English glosses (pp. 51–66) by the same English words. The picture pages are Latin only, so the ones that bear on these records were read through: the kitchen and food (pp. 5–10), electronics (pp. 14–15), games (pp. 16–17), clothing, vehicles, and the town (pp. 38–41). Pages 5 and 9 were looked at as pictures (*rādula* is a grater, not a peeler).
- **Levi–Bini**: the supplement (printed pp. 1–68) by Italian headword, for about a hundred words (*affettare, autoritratto, baccalà, collaudo, controllo, rosario, rubinetto, spatola, stivaletto, utente*, and the like). It has little for these records.
- **The Latin** of each term in Lewis & Short (`ls`), to see whether the "coinage" is there in this sense or a near one.
- Traupman's computer terms (pp. 261–262) and Morgan–Owens's sections on television and computers were read through for the computing records.

No claim below or in the records rests on anything else. Where a comment says that a reference work has no word, it means these searches found none.

The outcomes are the first pass's, with one more, which the first pass did not need: **an ordinary combination kept as the record's term**. The first pass moved such a phrase to the examples because an attested term stood beside it. Here there is none, and a record must have a term, so the phrase stays, still marked ours, and its comment says that it is an ordinary combination or description and what the reference works lack. This follows the first pass's *mēnsa pōmāria* (fruit stall) and *ōris tegimentum* (face mask). The line between this and "kept as needed" is not sharp: a term is counted here when anyone would put the same attested words together in their ordinary senses (*jūs legendī*, *summum tabulātum*, *autoraeda lūsōria*), and as needed when it is a longer description of ours, a stretched classical word, or a new formation.

A classical word used in its own sense, whose only novelty is the modern thing it is applied to, is relabelled with its period, as the editor ruled for *mendum* and *ēmendāre* on 14 November (*culcita* for a padded mat, *scrūtātiō* for a search, *jūs* for a permission). A classical word stretched to a new sense stays ours (*scrīptum* for a computer script, *ācroāma* for a podcast, *renovāre* for updating).

## For the editor: the doubtful terms

Not changed in the pass. Each line gives our word, the attested alternative with its source, and a recommendation. The editor ruled on all of them on 16 November 2026: under each case stand his ruling, in his words, and what was done. The Latin of every change was read that day in Lewis & Short.

- **oceanarium**: *ōceanārium* vs *piscīna zōologica*, a public aquarium (Andresian p. 43; no other source), and the modern *aquārium*, which the aquarium record has without a source. Recommendation: keep ours as the Latin form of the name of the thing in Lisbon, and say so in its comment; or drop the record and let *aquarium* serve.
  - **Ruled** (Jack, 16 November 2026): “keep ōceanārium as the Latin name of the one in Lisbon”. **Done:** the term is unchanged and still ours; its gloss and comment now say that it is the Latin form of the name of the Oceanarium in Lisbon, and the comment gives Andresian's *piscīna zōologica* (p. 43) as her word for a public aquarium in general.
- **pole (to hold on to)**: *columna* vs *pertica*, a pole or long staff (Traupman 49; Plautus, Ovid, Pliny in Lewis & Short), which the other record headed *pole* (`pole-stick`) has. Recommendation: keep *columna*: the pole in a carriage is a fixed upright, as a *columna* is, and a *pertica* is a loose pole that one carries; the comment should say so. The word stands in a reader.
  - **Ruled** (Jack, 16 November 2026): “pertica”. **Done:** *pertica, perticae f.* is the recommended term, labelled classical in a new sense as *mendum* is (Plin. NH 15.11 and Ov. F. 3.117 in Lewis & Short; Traupman 49). *Columna* stays as an alternative, still ours, because the record's one example has it (*columnam tenēns*); its comment gives the reason, that the pole in a carriage is a fixed upright.
- **salt cod**: *asellus sale condītus et siccātus* vs *merlūcius* (Levi–Bini p. 8, for *baccalà*; not in Lewis & Short). Recommendation: keep ours: Levi–Bini's word names the fish, in a form no dictionary of ours has, and not the salted and dried thing. It could be added as a modern alternative.
  - **Ruled** (Jack, 16 November 2026): “merlūcius”. **Done:** *merlūcius, merlūciī m.* is the recommended term, labelled modern with Levi–Bini p. 8 as its evidence, as the lexicon enters their other words; our description is a composed example. The Italian headword is *baccalà*, which is salt cod, and the book has a different word for the hake (*nasello*: *merula*, p. 42), so the ruling holds. Levi–Bini print *merlucius, ii* without marks and the word is not in Lewis & Short. The long *ū* is that of *lūcius*, the pike (Lewis & Short, from Ausonius), and holds if the name is “sea pike”; the ETYFish Project, read that day, gives that derivation beside another from *merula* and calls the etymology uncertain. A note on the record says so. The comment adds that zoology gives the name to the hake (Linnaeus's *Gadus merluccius*).
- **slice (cut into slices)**: *in segmenta secāre* vs *in frusta dissecāre* (Levi–Bini p. 2, for *affettare*). Recommendation: keep ours, since *segmentum* is the slice in Traupman (pp. 73, 380) and Morgan–Owens, and *frusta* are pieces; add Levi–Bini's phrase as a modern alternative.
  - **Ruled** (Jack, 16 November 2026): “theirs first”. **Done:** Levi–Bini's *in frusta dissecāre* (p. 2, for *affettare*) is the recommended term, labelled modern; ours, *in segmenta secāre*, is the alternative, with its reason (*segmentum* is the slice of Traupman and Morgan–Owens). The lexicon has no entry for *dissecō*, so that word of the term is not linked.
- **source code**: *cōdex fontālis*. *Fontālis* is not in Lewis & Short, though the comment calls it a post-classical adjective; Lewis & Short has *fontānus* and *fontāneus* (of a spring), and Morgan–Owens *fōns* for a source of information. No reference work has a term for source code. Recommendation: keep the coinage, but correct the comment, or find *fontālis* in a dictionary of later Latin first.
  - **Ruled** (Jack, 16 November 2026): “keep it; find a source for fontālis or correct the comment”. **Done:** the term is kept. *Fontālis* is in none of the reference works (Lewis & Short, de Vaan, Traupman, Morgan–Owens, Levi–Bini, Andresian, each searched for “fontal”); Morgan–Owens has *fōns* for a source of information and for an origin (s.v. source), and nothing for source code. On the web two things were read: the *New English Dictionary* s.v. fontal (in the transcription at wehd.com) derives the English word from “med.L. *fontālis*”, and Latdict enters *fontālis* from Stelten's *Dictionary of Ecclesiastical Latin* (1995), which was not itself read. So the comment now calls the adjective medieval, not post-classical, and cites the *New English Dictionary*. Du Cange could not be opened from here.
- **tray (at a security check)**: *cista*, a box or basket, vs *alveolus*, a tray or trough (Phaedrus 2.5, Livy 28.45, in Lewis & Short), *ferculum* (Traupman 73, 125, the record *tray*), and *repositōrium* (Morgan–Owens s.v. dish: tray). Recommendation: *alveolus*, which is the shallow tub the thing is, with *cista* as the alternative; but *cista* stands in a reader, so the choice is the editor's.
  - **Ruled** (Jack, 16 November 2026): “alveolus; cista as alternative”. **Done:** *alveolus, alveolī m.* is the recommended term, labelled classical in a new sense as *mendum* is (Phaedr. 2.5; Liv. 28.45, in Lewis & Short); *cista* is the alternative, still ours, and keeps the example that has it. The builder now shows *alveolus* in the family of *alveus*, which Lewis & Short bears out.
- **vitaminic**: *vītamīnicus*, an adjective with no source, for an English word hardly in use. Traupman has only the noun *vītamīnum* (pp. 66, 402). Recommendation: delete the record; the genitive (*vītamīnōrum*) or *vītamīna continēns* does the work, as the multivitamin record shows.
  - **Remark** (Jack, 16 November 2026); he chose nothing and wrote: “can we find something?”. **Searched** for “vitamin” in every reference: Traupman has the noun *vītamīnum* (p. 66; p. 402 prints *vitamīnum*); Morgan–Owens has *vitamina, ae* f. (s.v. vitamin, after modern Greek) and two headings without Latin (food supplement, mineral); Levi–Bini have neither *vitamina* nor *vitaminico*; Andresian, Lewis & Short, and de Vaan have nothing. No source has an adjective. **Done:** the record is rebuilt on Traupman's noun: its answer is the genitive *vītamīnōrum*, labelled modern, with a comment that Latin says “of vitamins” (as environmental is answered by *nātūrae*). *Vītamīnicus* is kept below it as an alternative of ours and its comment says that no reference work has it. Whether to delete it is for the editor; I would.

Things done that the editor may want to reverse or take further:

- **my turn**: *Nunc mē ōrdō vocat* is Traupman's sentence for *your turn* (p. 399) with the pronoun changed, labelled modern, as the first pass did for *your turn*. *Nunc ego!* is now an example.
- **play together**: *collūdere* is now the term. Lewis & Short calls it very rare in the classical authors (Horace, A. P. 159); it is the ordinary word of the school colloquia (Schottennius, in the loci collections), and *collūsor*, a playmate, is in Cicero and Pliny. If a rare word should not lead, *ūnā lūdere* can return as the term and *collūdere* stand as the alternative.
- **sauerkraut**: *brassica acida* (Scheller, in Morgan–Owens) is recommended and *holus acidum* is the alternative, although Morgan–Owens puts the second first, with Pliny (NH 26.88) and André behind it. We could not read the passage in Pliny, and "a sour vegetable" does not name the cabbage. If Pliny bears it out, the order should turn round.
- **steam (cook with steam)**: *vapōrāre* is Morgan–Owens's verb (s.v. boil: steam, citing Pliny). In Pliny it is to expose a thing to steam (*oculōs vapōrārī*, NH 28.170, Lewis & Short), not to cook. It is labelled classical as a word; the kitchen use is Morgan–Owens's.
- **the relabellings as classical** (*prōpōnere* for posting, *jūs* for a permission, *culcita* for a padded mat, *scrūtātiō* for a security check, *fremere* and *fremitus* for an engine's roar, *mūtus* for a phone on silent) follow the ruling on *mendum*: the word keeps its own sense and only the thing is new. Each comment says that the application is modern or ours. On the site they lose their asterisk. If the asterisk should stay wherever the application is ours, these seven go back.
- **network**: *rēte* is labelled modern because Traupman gives *rēte* for "net" among his computer terms (p. 261). He may mean the internet and not any network.
- **pitched roof**: Festus is labelled classical, as six of the eight terms that already cite him are.
- **podcast**: the bare loanword *podcast* was left as an alternative beside *ācroāma*. The first pass deleted the bare loanword *emoji*; this one may go the same way. *Wi-Fi* under *network* is a name and was kept.
  - **Remark** (Jack, 16 November 2026); he chose nothing and wrote: “acroama is the word used by all latin podcasters”. **Done:** *ācroāma, ācroāmatis n.* is labelled classical in a new sense, as *mendum* is, with Lewis & Short's citations (Plin. Ep. 6.31.13; Suet. Vesp. 19) and Nepos, Atticus 14.1, which Lewis & Short quotes under a wrong reference to Cicero and which was read in a text. The comment says that it is the word Latin podcasters use, and two are cited from pages read that day: *Legio XIII* (its episode notes on Apple Podcasts: *seriēs acroāmatum*) and *Quid Es Tu?* (its description: *de Acroamate*). The bare loanword *podcast* is deleted, as *emoji* was. One doubt, for the session on vowel lengths: the record keeps Lewis & Short's *ācrŏāmă* with a long first vowel, but the Greek is ἀκρόαμα, Lewis & Short itself prints *ăcrŏchordon* and *ăcrŏpŏdĭum*, and *Legio XIII* writes *acroāmatum* with a short one. The record's source line “No source has a word for a podcast” was left for the session rewriting the sources; beside the two podcasts it should read “No reference work”.
- **vegetable stall**: kept as it stood, with the comment the fruit stall got on 14 November. Morgan–Owens s.v. produce stand prints *taberna holerârium*, which is not a possible phrase as printed; it was not entered.
- **tester**: *probātor* is the Vulgate's "trier, examiner" (Lewis & Short) and is labelled late; *probātrīx* is not in Lewis & Short and stays ours.

## Noticed on the way, outside this pass

- **rubber surface**: the term writes *gummī* with a long final vowel; Lewis & Short has *gummi* or *cummi*, indeclinable, unmarked. Rubber in Morgan–Owens and Traupman is *cummis Indica* and *cummeus*. For the pass on macron slips.
- **shear attachment**: *accessōrium* is not in Lewis & Short or in the modern references; the comment now says so. Traupman's *appendicula* (p. 260) is an e-mail attachment.
- **rubber boots**: *calceī altī cummeī* does not use the words of the record *boots* (*pērō*, *ocreae*, *caliga*).
- **pepper relish** uses *piperītis* for the pepper, which the bell pepper record lists as an alternative to *capsicum*.
- Three comments carry dates or speak of earlier conversations, against the rule that an entry's text has neither: quality assurance ("our 21 September 2026 conversation"), smoothie ("corrected on 23 October 2026"), reproduce a bug ("the earlier QA conversation"). They were left as they are; only a sentence on what the references lack was added.
- `sources` lines that describe the search ("No source has a word for…", "Searched in…", "Descriptions and prompts labelled as ours…") were left for the session that is removing them. Where this pass entered an attested term such a line may now be untrue of the record (*mould*, *roll*, *post*). Lines were only added here, and a Traupman line extended (rubber boots), so the two sets of changes touch the same `sources` lists.
- Two source lines link pages of neolatinlexicon.org for words that are not in the copy of the *Adumbratio* in `refs/`: "Morgan–Owens: screwdriver" (powered screwdriver; the *Adumbratio* has the heading with no Latin) and "Morgan–Owens: spatula" (skimming blade; the *Adumbratio* has *rudicula*).
- The aquarium record's *aquārium* is labelled modern without evidence.
- *metro* (*ferrivia subterrānea*, Traupman) and *metro train* (*trāmen subterrāneum*, Morgan–Owens) are now both attested; Morgan–Owens gives its phrase for the subway as a whole, so the two records may be one.
- `answer_kinds.py` lists two of these records, as it did before the pass: *sunscreen* (it takes *sōlāre* for a verb) and *vroom* (an interjection answered by a verb, which the comment explains).
- Morgan–Owens has further words these records did not need: *chīramaxium* (Petronius) and *plōstellum* for a pushcart, *culcita īnflātilis* for an air mattress (also Traupman 124), *vatillum* for a scoop.
