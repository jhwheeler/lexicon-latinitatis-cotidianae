# Feelings and behaviour, shopping and money, the phone, work, and computers: the sweep of 23 November 2026

Jack's sixth to eighth categories (23 November 2026), after getting around, eating, play, the day with a child, and the weather. About 520 English phrases were checked against the lexicon; some 315 were already answered, and the gaps were researched in seven groups (feelings; correcting a child; blame, promises, and encouragement; in the shop; more shopping and money; the phone and computers; work).

35 records are new and 119 gained English wordings, examples, terms, or all three. All are drafts.

## How it was done

- As in the sweeps before: the authors first (Lewis & Short, the loci collections, the Latin Library texts), then Traupman, Morgan–Owens, and Levi–Bini for modern things, then a plain description.
- Feelings and behaviour come almost wholly from Plautus and Terence (*Nē timē!*, *Nē plōrā!*, *Nē fēcerīs!*, *Quotiēns dīcendum est tibi?*, *Nōn ego.*), with Cicero's letters, Horace, Seneca's *De ira* on the tempers of children, and Quintilian. Shopping and money: Plautus's markets and bankers, Cicero's letters to Atticus, Horace, Martial, Petronius. Work: Pliny's letters, Cicero, Martial 4.8. The phone and the computer are modern, and their words build on the lexicon's own (*compellāre*, *accumulātrum*, *interrēte*, *quadrum*).
- Every quotation was matched word for word, by script, against the Latin Library texts or found in the loci tables. Many line numbers of Plautus and Terence were counted from the nearest printed number of the text file, across split verses; they want checking against a numbered edition at review.
- Quotations from grim or indecent scenes were left out where a plain one would do (the sale of a girl, a threat of suicide, Juvenal's *sīc volō, sīc jubeō*, which is a demand to crucify a slave). A few lines whose words are harmless stand though their speaker is not (Ballio's *Negōtī nunc sum plēnus*, Plaut. Ps. 380).
- The school colloquies were not used.

## Terms that are ours

Nothing attested was found for these, so a description or a sentence of ours leads, with what was searched in its comment:

- error message: *nūntius mendī*
- I'm just looking: *Īnspiciō modo.* (on the buyer's verb *īnspicere*, Plaut. Most. 753–754)
- inbox: *scrīnium litterārum acceptārum*
- loyalty card: *tabella fidēlitātis*, with *tessera* as the classical alternative
- missed call: *tēlephōnēma praetermissum*
- web page: *pāgina interrētiālis*
- working hours: *hōrae negōtiōsae* (on Tacitus's *negōtiōsī diēs*, Ann. 13.41)

Classical words used of a modern thing, labelled classical with the new sense in the gloss, as the ruling of 16 November provides; Jack has not been asked about these:

- settings: *optiōnēs*; tab (in a browser): *scheda*; zoom in and out: *amplificāre*, *contrahere*; unplug: *disjungere*; signal (of a phone): *signum*
- price tag: *pittacium* (Petronius 34), with *titulus*; fitting room: *apodȳtērium*, the baths' undressing room
- withdraw money: *dēprōmere* (Cicero, of money from the treasury); transfer money: *permūtāre* (Cicero's remitting by exchange)
- market stall: *mēnsa* (Lewis & Short: a stand in the market); piggy bank: *loculī* (Hor. Ep. 2.1.175)

## For Jack to judge

- **go to work**: *ad officium īre* leads, from Pliny (Ep. 3.5.9), where *officium* is a post or duty; the lexicon's word for the room is *officīna*.
- **what's your job?**: the record still leads with Traupman's *Quem quaestum facis?*; in Plautus *quaestum facere* is often a courtesan's trade. *Quam artem exercēs?* (on Hor. Ep. 1.14.44) was added and could lead.
- **funny**: *rīdiculus* now leads; *jocōsus* and *fēstīvus* ("witty, full of jokes") are alternatives.
- **calm down**: *Quiēsce.* (Plautus, Terence) was added as an alternative; the record's *Tē continē.* still leads.
- **because I said so**: *Quia libet.* (Ter. And. 862) is nearer "because I feel like it"; *Nōlō, inquam.* stands for "no means no".
- **that's naughty**: *Male facis.* and *improbē*; **answer back**: *respōnsāre* rests on Plaut. Men. 620–621 (and Horace's *respōnsāre cupīdinibus*, defy).
- **don't be afraid**: *Nē timē!* leads; *Nōlī timēre!* was found as such only in the Vulgate, though the construction is classical.
- **frustrated, sulk, in a bad mood**: all went under *stomachārī*; **giggle** has no word, and stands under laugh with *rīsum tenēre nōn posse*.
- **I'm counting to three**: *Ūnum … duo … cavē nē «tria» dīcam!*; we have found no classical *numerāre ūsque ad* with a number.
- **say thank you, what do you say?**: the prompts are ours; we have found no ancient parent prompting a child so.
- **freelance**: *operam suam locāre* (Plaut. Trin. 843–844) is any hiring-out of one's work; the "for whoever I like" is in the examples.
- **commute**: *commeāre* (Ter. Hec. 175, *hūc rārō in urbem commeat*).
- **add to basket** (online): *in carrulum conjicere*, with the lexicon's word for the shopping trolley.
- **it's not working**: *nōn fungitur*, used absolutely, is modern, as the record "work" says; *officiō suō nōn fungitur* is the classical construction.
- **bite**: the record is filed under food; a child biting someone uses the same verb, and no second record was made.

## What's your job?

Jack (23 November 2026): Latin speakers online ask for one's *officium*, and sometimes one's *mūnus*. The question has its own record now, taken out of "employment": *Quod est officium tuum?* leads, as it is said today (*officium* is a post from Caesar on; of any job the use is modern), with *Quō mūnere fungeris?* (on Cicero's and Pliny's *mūnere fungī*), *Quam artem exercēs?*, and Traupman's *Quem quaestum facis?*, which keeps its warning. Which of the first two should lead is for Jack to say.

## The line numbers of Plautus and Terence

On 23 November 2026 every reference to Plautus and Terence by line in the lexicon (2,172 of them, in evidence lines and examples) was compared by script with the numbers the Latin Library texts print at every fifth verse.

- 786 agree with the text's own numbering.
- 15 records had a number the text's numbering shows to be wrong, most by one line and eight of them in the *Eunuchus*; they are corrected (advice, at-least, at-the-house-of, cell-room, don't do that, I'm talking to you, interpretation, it makes no difference, mind, nothing in excess, shh, sometimes, sure, think, world).
- 729 stand where the text file breaks verses over two lines, so that counting from the printed numbers cannot settle them, and 626 could not be found by their opening words (older records that cite by act and scene, quotations with omissions, other readings). These still want a numbered edition; none was open to this session.
