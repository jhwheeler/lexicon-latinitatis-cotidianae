# Getting around: the sweep of 22 November 2026

Jack asked (22 November 2026), after "around the corner" went in, for the other phrases of that kind that come up every day: finding the way, going about a town, travelling. About 380 English phrases were checked against the lexicon; some 180 were already answered, and the gaps were researched in six groups (asking and giving directions; position along a route; walking with a child; places on the way; trips and transport; tickets, rooms, and trouble). The research was done by report-only agents and vetted by the coordinating session; nothing here is Jack's review.

56 records are new and 57 gained English wordings, examples, or both. All are drafts.

## How it was done

- For each phrase: what the authors say in the same situation (Lewis & Short, the loci collections, the Latin Library texts), then Traupman, Morgan–Owens, and Levi–Bini for modern things, then a plain description.
- Every quotation was matched word for word, by script, against the Latin Library texts (macrons, j, and v apart). The few that did not match were looked at one by one: an edition's spelling (*paullulum*), a run-together word in the file, or a quotation taken from Lewis & Short, which carries the mark.
- Where a section number could not be confirmed, the passage is cited by chapter only (Livy, Caesar, and some of Cicero's letters).
- An ordinary combination of attested words stands among the examples as composed, not as a term.

## Terms that are ours

Nothing attested was found for these, so a description or a sentence of ours leads, marked ours, with what was searched in its comment:

- is this the train to …? *Petitne hoc trāmen …?*
- it takes an hour: *Hōrae iter est.* (Livy's *id diēī iter est* with an hour)
- jet lag: *lassitūdō ex hōrārum discrīmine*
- look both ways: *Circumspice, nē quid veniat.* (on Plautus's *circumspice, nē quis …*)
- mind the step: *Cavē gradum.* (on *cavē canem*)
- pedestrian street: *via peditum*
- roundabout: *compitum rotundum*
- street name: *nōmen viae*
- take the first street on the left: *Prīmam viam ad sinistram ingredere.*
- when is the next train? *Quandō proximum trāmen discēdet?* (Traupman's question with *proximum*)
- which way is it to …? *Quā ītur ad …?*

## For Jack to judge

- **Classical words stretched to modern travel.** *assequī* for catching a bus (classically, catching someone up by hurrying); *raedā tollere* (Hor. S. 2.6.42) for giving a lift; *numerus* for a house number (Levi–Bini's addresses).
- **Missing a bus or train** went into the existing record for missing a plane (*ad … sērius venīre*, Traupman), not under *āmittere*, which would follow Italian and Spanish.
- **a stone's throw**: *tēlī jactus* (Vergil, Livy) leads; Apuleius's *lapidis jactus* is the same image as the English and stands second.
- **landmark**: *nota*, on one phrase of Cicero's (*signa et notae locōrum*, de Or. 2.174).
- **viewpoint**: *specula* leans towards a watch-post.
- **ramp**: *ascēnsus* is any way up; no word that is specially a ramp was found.
- **old town**: Varro's *antīquum oppidum* is the first settlement on the Palatine; its use of a historic quarter is ours.
- **main street** has *platēa*, which is also the answer under avenue; **waterfront** has *lītus*, which is also under shore.
- **how storeys are counted**: Traupman counts the American way and Levi–Bini the Italian, so their *tabulātum secundum* is one floor apart; the comment under storey says so and offers Martial's *scālīs habitō tribus*.
- **how long does it take?** leads with Cicero's *Quot diērum via est?*; the child's *Quam mox perveniēmus?* is ours and stands as an alternative.
- **we're almost there**: *Jam prope est* is a sentence of ours on Plautus's *prope est*.

## Left out

- Words from the school colloquies (Corderius, Erasmus) and Franmorarius, a Greek coinage for a one-way street (*monodromus*), and Fronto's *apopsis* for a viewpoint.
- Section numbers given from memory.
- Not done: *crypta* as a term for a road tunnel (Sen. Ep. 57), *dēversārī* under spend the night, and *sub colle* as a term under the foot of a mountain. Several records that were extended still carry a source line naming the readers; that is for the pass on reader references.
