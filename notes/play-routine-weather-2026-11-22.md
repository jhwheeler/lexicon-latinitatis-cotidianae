# Play, the day with a child, and the weather: the sweep of 22 November 2026

Jack's third, fourth, and fifth categories (22 November 2026), after getting around and eating: play and the playground; the daily routine with a child (getting up, dressing, the bath, the toilet, bedtime, getting out of the door); the weather and the outdoors. About 400 English phrases were checked against the lexicon; some 205 were already answered, and the gaps were researched in six groups (outdoor play; indoor play; the morning and the bath; bedtime and leaving; the weather; out of doors).

27 records are new and 93 gained English wordings, examples, terms, or all three. All are drafts.

## How it was done

- As in the two sweeps before: the authors first (Lewis & Short, the loci collections, the Latin Library texts), then Traupman, Morgan–Owens, and Levi–Bini for modern things, then a plain description.
- Much comes from the comedies (Plautus's *Rudens*, *Persa*, *Mostellaria*, *Curculio*; Terence's *Heauton timorumenos*), Cato and Varro on the farm, Horace's *Satires* and *Epistles* on children's games, Seneca (*De constantia* 12 on children at play; the *Naturales quaestiones*), Vergil's *Georgics* for the sky and the weather, Pliny's letters, and Suetonius on Augustus's nights.
- Every quotation was matched word for word, by script, against the Latin Library texts or found in the loci tables. Where the Latin Library's reading differs from the loci table's (Verg. G. 1.366, *praecipitēs*), the loci table's stands.
- The school colloquies were not used.

## Terms that are ours

Nothing attested was found for these, so a description or a sentence of ours leads, with what was searched in its comment:

- cartwheel: *in modum rotae sē volvere*
- jigsaw puzzle: *pictūra dissecta*; a piece that fits: *convenit* (Cato, Cicero)
- pillow fight: *pugna pulvīnōrum*
- play nicely: *sine rixā lūdere*; *placidē lūdere*
- ready, steady, go: *Parātī? Currite!*; on your marks: *Ad carcerēs!* (the circus's stalls, Cicero's figure in Sen. 83)
- skim stones: *testās per summam aquam jaculārī* (the game is described by Minucius Felix, Oct. 3, who is post-classical)
- snowflake: *floccus nivis* (on Martial's fleece of silent waters, 4.3)
- tug of war: *certāmen fūnis trahendī*
- you're it (tag): *Tetigī tē! Tū nunc sequere!*
- no cheating: *Nōlī fallere!*, beside the classical *Nōn aequum facis.*

Conker is *castanea equīna*, Mattioli's name of 1571 (Morgan–Owens), marked renaissance.

## For Jack to judge

- **it looks like rain**: Plautus's *Pluet.* (Curc. 132, with *crēdō*) now leads, and Traupman's *Vidētur quasi pluat.* is the alternative. This changes an existing answer, by the rule that a classical saying leads.
- **echo**: *imāgō* (Vergil, Horace; Lewis & Short's "pure Latin" word) was added as an alternative. The record's answer is still *Ēchō*; *imāgō* could lead.
- **sunburn**: *sōle adustus* of the person leads; we have found no ancient noun for a burn from the sun, and *adustiō* (Pliny) is any burn.
- **that's cheating**: *Nōn aequum facis.* (Terence, Plautus) is "that's not fair", the nearest classical protest found.
- **I give up**: *Manūs dō.* (Plautus, Horace, Cicero). Festus's *herbam dō* is mentioned in the comment only.
- **did you sleep well?**: *Bene quiēvistī?* The evidence for *bene quiēscere* of a night's sleep includes Arria's answer about her dead son (Plin. Ep. 3.16), a sad context for a morning greeting; Plaut. Merc. 370 stands beside it.
- **the days are getting shorter**: *Diēs dēcrēscunt.* and *Diēs crēscunt.* are the finite forms of Pliny's ablative absolutes (NH 2.151) and Lucretius's infinitive; we have found no instance in the indicative.
- **shooting star**: *stēlla cadēns*, from Seneca's *stēllae … cadentēs* (NQ 7.23.2), which may be a description more than a name.
- **let's pretend, I'll be the doctor**: *medicum agere*, the actor's *agere* (Cicero, Tacitus) extended to a child's part.
- **tuck in**: *operīre*, "cover"; we have found no word for tucking the bedclothes in.
- **get some fresh air**: the composed example has *sub dīvum* with motion, which rests on Horace (C. 1.18.13) alone.
- **count to ten** (hide and seek): the example says *decem numerā*; we have found no classical *numerāre ūsque ad* with a number.
- **spin round, swing me round**: the examples went to "turn" (transitive *circumagere*); spinning round oneself (*sē circumagere*, *circumagī*) may want a record of its own.
- **toy**: the examples use the record's word, *lūdibrium* (Traupman). Classically it is "mockery, a plaything of fortune"; Morgan–Owens ranks *lūdicrum* higher. Not changed, in case it is the household's word.
- **cartwheel**: the somersault record links a Morgan–Owens page headed "somersaults / do somersaults / turn cartwheels", which could not be read in this session. If it gives a word for the cartwheel, that word should replace our description.

## Quantities

- *adustus* with a short *u*, as in the hub's ruling of 11 November (de Vaan's *ustum*; Bennett's *ūstus* rests on the Romance forms). The record “salty” still has *adūstum* in a quotation of Terence; one of the two should give way.
- *ōscitō* with a long *ō*, as the lexicon already writes it (from *ōs*); Lewis & Short leaves it unmarked.
- *plōstellum* with a long *ō* (a diminutive of *plōstrum*, *plaustrum*); Lewis & Short leaves it unmarked.
- *crābrō*, *crābrōnum* with a long *ā* (Lewis & Short).

## Jack's answers (23 November 2026)

- **toy**: *lūdicrum* leads (Catullus 61.24; Morgan–Owens's first word), and it is the word he uses at home; Traupman's *lūdibrium* is the alternative. The composed examples that said *lūdibria* of toys (lighter, shop window, tidy up, vacuum) now say *lūdicra*.
- **tug of war, hide and seek**: the Greek names stand as alternatives, in Latin letters and marked ours, since we have found no Latin author who uses them: *helcystinda lūdere* (ἑλκυστίνδα, διελκυστίνδα; Pollux 9.112, Eustathius 1111.24) and *apodidrāscinda lūdere* (ἀποδιδρασκίνδα; Pollux 9.117).
- **cartwheel**: Jack read the Morgan–Owens page: *cernuāre* is the somersault (Apuleius), *corpus rotāre* the cartwheel (German *radschlagen*; Bauer, Helfer). *Corpus rotāre* leads, and our description is the alternative.
- **skim stones**: he knows it as skipping stones; "skipping stones", "skip rocks", and their like are among the record's wordings.
- **did you sleep well?**: stays as it is; Plautus has the verb of a night's sleep too.
- **a classical saying leads over a modern one**: agreed in general, with respect for what Latin speakers use today (now in `docs/drafting.md`).
- **chew**: *mandō, mandere* leads (Cicero, Pliny); *mandūcō*, the everyday lengthened form that became the Romance word for eating, is the alternative, with Augustus's letter (Suet. Aug. 76).
- **echo**: *imāgō* leads (Vergil, Horace); the Greek *Ēchō* is the alternative.
