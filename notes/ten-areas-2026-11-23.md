# Ten more areas of everyday life: the sweep of 23 November 2026

Jack, 23 November 2026, shown the gaps sampled in ten further areas: "it seems like we could do it all in one batch". The areas: health and being ill; pets, hens, and the garden; talking about language; housework and repairs; social occasions; flights, hotels, and the beach; the car; time and plans; early learning (letters, numbers, shapes); and a few conversation fillers. 659 English phrases were checked against the lexicon; 361 were already answered, and the gaps were researched in eight groups.

29 records are new and 173 gained English wordings, examples, terms, or all three. All are drafts.

## How it was done

- As in the sweeps before: the authors first (Lewis & Short, the loci collections, the Latin Library texts), then Traupman, Morgan–Owens, and Levi–Bini for modern things, then a plain description.
- Health: Plautus's patients (*Animō male est*, *numquid tibi dolet?*, *pallēs*), Cicero's letters to the sick Tiro (Fam. 16), Martial, Seneca. Animals and the garden: Cato, Varro (book 3), Columella (books 7, 8, 11), Vergil's *Georgics*, Phaedrus. Language: Cicero (*bene Latīnē*), Gellius, Quintilian, Seneca Ep. 58, the interpreter scene of the *Poenulus*, Ovid in exile. Housework: Plautus's lists of household jobs (Ps. 157–164, Stich. 62–65, 347–359), Cato Agr. 143, Juvenal 14.59–65. Social occasions: Plautus and Terence, Cicero's letters, Catullus 13, Tibullus on birthdays. Time: Plautus, Terence, Cicero's letters. Learning: Quintilian 1.1 and 1.10, Horace, Seneca Ep. 94.
- Every quotation was matched word for word, by script, against the Latin Library texts or found in the loci tables. Five that could not be found in the texts were left out (Plin. NH 15.82; Cic. Q. fr. 2.6.1; Ter. Hec. 140; Cic. Fam. 7.26.1; and one whose reference had no section). Where the loci table and the Latin Library differ (Phaedr. 4.3.4, *Nōndum mātūra est*; Ter. And. 310, *ut libet*), the loci table's reading stands.
- Line numbers of Plautus and Terence were read from the numbers the text files print; where a verse is broken over two lines between the marks, the number is counted and wants checking against a numbered edition.
- Quotations from grim or indecent scenes were left out where a plain one would do.
- The school colloquies were not used.

## Terms that are ours

- how do you say it in Latin?: *Quōmodo hoc Latīnē dīcitur?* We have found no classical instance of the question itself; it is built on Seneca's *quōmodo dīcētur …?* (Ep. 58.6) and Cicero's *Latīnē dīcere*, which stand beside it as alternatives.
- baggage reclaim: *locus sarcinārum recipiendārum*
- snorkel: *fistula spīritālis*

Classical words used of a modern thing, labelled classical with the new sense in the gloss, as the ruling of 16 November provides; Jack has not been asked about these:

- turbulence: *jactātiō* (a ship's tossing; Tertullian's *turbulentia* is marked "avoid")
- litter tray: *alveolus*; bird feeder: *canāliculus* (Columella's little troughs of millet); pet food: *cibāria*
- drain: *cloāca*, with *ēmissārium*; leak: *mānāre*, and *perpluere* of a roof
- line (in a drawing): *līnea*

Flu is *febris catarrhālis* (modern medical Latin, Morgan–Owens); rectangle is *quadrātum oblongum* (Mercuriale, 1569, Morgan–Owens), with Vitruvius's adjective *oblongus* and the modern *rēctangulum* beside it; synonym is *synōnymum* (post-classical: Fronto, Priscian), with Quintilian's *verbum idem significāns*.

## A classical saying now leads (Jack's rule of 23 November)

- I feel better: *Melius mihi est.* (Cic. Fam. 16.22, Att. 6.7.2) leads; *Melius mē habeō.* is an alternative.
- I haven't got time: *Ōtium nōn est.* (Plaut. Merc. 374) leads; *Tempus nōn est mihi.* is an alternative.
- as you wish: *Ut libet.* (Ter. And. 310) leads; Traupman's *Estō, ut lubet!* is an alternative.

## For Jack to judge

- **nice to meet you**: we have found no classical formula for a first meeting. Plautus's *volup est convēnisse* (Mil. 276–277), the source of Traupman's *Tē convēnisse volup est*, is said to someone already known.
- **happy birthday, happy Easter**: Tibullus's lines are prayers to the birthday god, not a greeting, so the modern wish still leads; "happy Easter" stands on the Church's greeting, with *Fēlīx tibi sit Pascha!* as a composed wish.
- **I'm sorry for your loss**: *Molestē ferō.* (Sen. Ep. 63.1; Cic. Fam. 4.5.1), with *Tuā causā doleō.* Only the gentle lines of the consolations are quoted.
- **would you like to come for dinner?**: the classical invitation is a statement, *Cēnābis apud mē.* (Catullus 13, Martial 11.52).
- **make yourself at home**: *Ēs, bibe, animō obsequere.* (Plaut. Mil. 677).
- **I feel faint**: *Animō male est.* (Plautus, four times); **get well soon** went under "take care" (*Cūrā ut valeās*, *Fac ut valeās*), as Cicero uses it of both.
- **I grazed my knee**: *atterere*, on Pliny's medical *attrīta* (through Lewis & Short); we have found no classical sentence for it.
- **telling the time**: the records disagree. "Telling the time" has *hōra tertia cum sēmisse, cum quadrante, cum dōdrante*; "hour", "quarter of an hour", and "three quarters of an hour" have Traupman's *et* forms. The new examples follow the first.
- **the car has broken down, the car won't start**: no reference work has a word; *vitium facere* and *mōtrum accendī nōn potest* are composed.
- **the fuse has blown**: no record was made; the only candidate was a coinage on a post-classical adjective.
- **air the room**: *perflāre* answers from the wind's side (*cubiculum perflātur*).
- **how many sleeps?**: *Quot noctēs adhūc dormiēmus?*, with Caesar and Tacitus on peoples who count by nights.
- **every Monday**: *singulīs diēbus Lūnae*, on Traupman's *singulīs hebdomadibus*.
- **purr**: *murmurāre* rests on Traupman alone; **lay eggs**: bare *parere* is the farm writers' word and was added beside *ōva parere*.
- **the circle has no record**: the shapes have square, triangle, star, and now rectangle, but not *orbis* or *circulus*.
