# Ten more areas: the second sweep of 23 November 2026

Jack, 23 November 2026, shown ten areas not yet swept: "Great, please do that." The areas: cooking and the table; cafés and restaurants; the body; clothes; animals and a forest walk; town and transport; stories and make-believe; music, drawing, and crafts; sport; feasts, birthdays, and church; the building site and tools; school and reading. 929 English phrases were checked against the lexicon; 699 were already answered, and the other 230 were researched in seven groups by Opus researchers (report only). Claude (Fable) vetted the reports and applied them.

34 records are new and 164 gained English wordings, examples, terms, or all three. All are drafts.

## Jack's rulings recorded here

- **Telling the time** (23 Nov): "we can have both sets". The twelfths with *cum* (*hōra tertia cum sēmisse*) and Traupman's quarter-hours with *et* (*hōrā septimā et sēmihōrā*) both stand. The telling-the-time record already carried both; it gains Traupman's half-past example, and hour gains *hōrā nōnā cum sēmisse* beside *et sēmihōrā*.
- **What's your job** (23 Nov): both *Quod est officium tuum?* and *Quō mūnere fungeris?* stand, as the record has them.
- **Computer words** (23 Nov): *optiōnēs*, *scheda*, *amplificāre / contrahere*, *disjungere*, and *signum* stay, "if we don't have other options".

## Terms that are ours

We have found no word in the authors, Traupman, Morgan–Owens, or Levi–Bini for these, so each is a plain description, marked ours:

- ponytail: *crīnēs religātī* (the poets' *religāre*; it covers a bun as well)
- unbutton: *globulōs solvere*; hoodie: *thōrāx cucullātus*; laundry basket: *corbis lavandāriōrum*
- they lived happily ever after: *Atque ex eō tempore beātē vīxērunt.* (Cicero's *beātē vīvere*; the ancient tellers end on the event, as Ovid's *illā cōnsenuēre casā*)
- do a handstand: *manibus stāre* (beside Cicero's *manibus ingredī*, to walk on one's hands); do push-ups: *sē bracchiīs sublevāre*
- Easter egg: *ōvum Paschāle*
- toolbox: *arca ferrāmentōrum*; workbench: *mēnsa fabrīlis*; sandpaper: *charta harēnāta*; cement mixer: *māchina harēnātō miscendō* (Traupman's *māchina mixtōria* is a kitchen mixer); roofer: *tēgulārum strūctor*

Classical words used of a modern or fairy-tale thing, labelled classical with the new sense in the gloss:

- elf: *faunus* (the nearest classical being; Petronius's *incubō* beside it)
- sticker: *pittacium* (Petronius's label on a wine jar)
- roller: *cylindrus*; window frame: *antepagmentum* (Cato, Vitruvius); floorboards: *axēs* (Vitruvius)
- magic wand: *virga* (Circe's and Mercury's); unicorn: *monocerōs* (Pliny), with the Vulgate's *ūnicornis*

## Calls for Jack to judge

- **Plait.** *crīnēs plexī* leads (the verb is classical of garlands; of hair we have found it only in the Vulgate, Judges 16.13, so it is marked late). Morgan–Owens's *capillus pāctilis*, which it gives with a query, is the alternative. The long *ā* follows the house rule for *pāctus* from *pangō*.
- **Goosebumps.** A verb for a noun: *pilī horrent* (Varro), with *inhorrēscere*.
- **The moral of the story.** Also a verb: *haec fābula significat*, *fābella admonet* (Phaedrus). We have found no classical noun for it.
- **Tadpole.** *gyrīnus* is in Pliny alone, but nothing else names the thing.
- **Roast.** Lewis & Short marks *assō* late (Apuleius, Apicius, the Vulgate). It still leads, as the cookery books' word and the one in use, now labelled late, with the classical *assus* (Horace) and *torreō* beside it. If the classical word should lead, they change places.
- **Felt-tip pen.** *calamus coāctilicius* is Morgan–Owens's, printed without quantities; the short *-icius* is our reading (an adjective made from *coāctilia*, not from a participle).
- **Right-handed.** Morgan–Owens's *dexterā manū agilior* is made on Suetonius's left-handed Tiberius; what one says is *Utrā manū scrībis? — Dextrā.*
- **Forward roll** rides on somersault (*cernuāre*); **Christmas carol** on hymn (*hymnus nātālīcius*, in an example only); **cast a spell** on magic; **paint a wall** on paint (*indūcere*, *dealbāre*).

## Dropped or changed in vetting

- Not found in the texts, so dropped: Ter. Haut. 587; Phaedr. 5.3.11 (which the loci table marks corrupt); Prudentius, Cath. 9.10–12; the full sentence of Cic. Att. 13.25.3, whose Greek word the researcher had restored (the word *syllabātim* stays, on Lewis & Short).
- Dropped for their setting: Cic. de Or. 2.234 (figurative), Plaut. Aul. 429–430 (an oath after a beating), Suet. Galb. 18 (an omen of death), Mart. 14.163 on the church bell (a bath bell; it stays on bell).
- Verg. G. 1.156 keeps its edition's *avīs*; Plaut. Stich. 221 takes the house's *vēndō*; Ter. Ad. 425 the house's *adustum*.
- Varro's Consualia is cited as LL 6.20 and Seneca's ball-players as Ben. 2.17.4, from memory of the editions' sections; the text files have none. Both want a look.
- tickle keeps its four examples, which a browser test counts; "it tickles" was added as a wording only.

## Slips fixed along the way

- tie: the example *Vincula calceōrum ligēmus* now has *corrigiās*, the lexicon's word for shoelaces.
- roast: *assō* was labelled classical.
- loose tooth: filed under the house; now under the body. Its two Morgan–Owens lines are one.
- coffee: two Traupman lines made one.
- Word families: *coaxātiō* (boarding) is kept apart from *coaxō* (to croak), and *poliō* from *polus*.

## Left open

These want a pass of their own.
- peel: *dēcorticō* leads, a word Lewis & Short gives of bark; Traupman's *cutem resecāre* may suit a potato better. lick: *lambō* leads; *lingō* may suit a child and a spoon better.
